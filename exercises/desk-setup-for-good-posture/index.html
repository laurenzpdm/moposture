Desk setup for good posture: a 9-point checklist

Neck and shoulders tight after a day at the computer? Before you add exercises, check the setup you sit in for hours. This checklist follows the guidance of the UK Health and Safety Executive (HSE) for display screen work, at home or in the office. Go through it once, top to bottom, in about two minutes.

General information, not medical advice. Move gently and stop if anything hurts.

The checklist

  1. Screen height. Top of the screen level with your eyes.
  2. Screen distance. About an arm's length away.
  3. Shoulders. Relaxed. Sit high enough that you do not need to shrug them.
  4. Keyboard. Just below elbow height.
  5. Seat height. Supports the front and back of your thighs equally. A cushion can raise you if needed.
  6. Back support. The backrest supports your lower back. A cushion can add support.
  7. Knee gap. A gap of 2 to 3 cm between the front of the seat and the back of your knee.
  8. Straight ahead. Computer and screen directly in front of you, screen and keyboard central, so you do not twist your back.
  9. Mouse. In line with your elbow.

Working on a laptop

A laptop puts the screen and keyboard together, so one of them is always in the wrong place. For longer use, HSE suggests:

  • a separate keyboard and mouse, so the laptop screen can be raised on a riser, a stand or a stack of books
  • or a separate display screen

How often to take breaks

HSE says there is no legal rule for how long or how often breaks should be, because it depends on the work. Its rule of thumb: short breaks often beat long breaks rarely, for example 5 to 10 minutes every hour rather than 20 minutes every 2 hours. Use the break to get up and move around, or at least to stretch and change posture.

Three quick moves for your break

All three work at the desk. Do them slowly and only within a range that feels comfortable.

Chin tuck

  1. Sit or stand tall and look straight ahead.
  2. Glide your chin straight back, as if making a double chin. Keep your eyes level.
  3. Hold for 3 seconds, then relax.

Dose: 8 repetitions.

Tip: Small and slow is fine. You should feel a light stretch at the base of your skull, never pain. Full guide.

Shoulder rolls

  1. Stand or sit tall with your arms relaxed.
  2. Lift your shoulders toward your ears, then roll them back and down in a big circle.
  3. After half the reps, reverse the direction.

Dose: 10 slow circles.

Tip: Good to do right after a long typing stretch. Full guide.

Chair back opener

  1. Sit on a sturdy chair with your upper back against the top of the backrest.
  2. Clasp your hands behind your head to support your neck.
  3. Gently lean back over the backrest, then come up.

Dose: 6 slow reps.

Tip: Lean only as far as feels good. Keep your ribs from flaring. Use a chair that does not roll or tip. Full guide.

Common mistakes

  • Fixing the chair but leaving the laptop flat on the desk. The screen then pulls your head down again.
  • Placing the screen off to one side, so you sit slightly twisted all day.
  • Sitting so low that you shrug to reach the keyboard.
  • Setting everything up once and then working for hours without getting up.

When to stop and see a professional

A better setup will not fix every pain. Speak to a doctor or physiotherapist if:

  • neck or back pain does not get better after a few weeks
  • you have numbness, tingling or weakness in an arm, hand or leg
  • pain spreads down an arm or leg
  • pain started after an accident, fall or other injury
